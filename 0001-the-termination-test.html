The termination test

Lesson 0001 · topic shape-up · mission: diagnose a planning system with Shape Up as the lens, and decide what to adopt without cargo-culting it.

Every planning system holds something still and lets something else move. Naming which is which takes about a minute and tells you more about the system than its documentation does. Shape Up is unusually useful as a lens here — not because its answers are right, but because it states its answers out loud, which most systems don't.

This lesson gives you three questions and one worked example. By the end you should be able to run them on a planning system you have never seen.

The instrument

Q1 — What is fixed, and what floats? Time, scope, quality: pick which one is nailed down. Singer's formulation is the crispest thing in the book:

Estimates start with a design and end with a number. Appetites start with a number and end with a design. — §1.2 Set Boundaries (R2)

Those run in opposite directions. An estimate takes scope as given and yields a duration; an appetite takes duration as given and forces scope to absorb the variance. So Q1 is not a vocabulary question. It asks which end of the problem the system refuses to negotiate.

Q2 — What makes work stop, other than being finished? Most systems have no answer. That is itself the answer, and it is worth writing down rather than glossing.

Q3 — On overrun, what is the default? Not what is permitted — what happens when nobody argues. Defaults are where a system's real policy lives, because they are what occurs under fatigue.Q3 is the load-bearing one. A system can have a beautiful Q1 answer and still never stop anything, because the default on overrun is "carry on".

Worked example: run it on Shape Up

Answers visible. Read this as a demonstration of the sort, not as facts to memorise.

Q1 → time is fixed; scope floats. The cycle is the constant. Singer works the example on the book you are reading: with one week left he can fix typos or add a section, so he cuts the section. His note on why is the mechanism — "Without the pressure of the fixed deadline, I wouldn't make the trade-off." (R2) The fixed end is not there to hurry anyone. It exists to force a choice that otherwise never gets made.

Q2 → the cycle boundary, plus a comparison rule. Work stops at six weeks, and the judgment of "good enough" is made by comparing down to baseline — what customers do today without this — rather than up to an ideal. (R1) Both halves matter: a deadline with no comparison rule just produces anxiety.

Q3 → cancel, and return to shaping. Overrun does not buy time; the project goes back to be re-shaped. (R3) But the book states two conditions under which it extends anyway, and this is where a slogan becomes a rule you can actually apply: the remaining work must be true must-haves that survived every attempt to scope-hammer them, and all of it must be downhill — no unsolved problems, no open questions. (R1 §3.5, "When to extend a project")

That second condition is the sharp one. Uphill work left at the deadline is read as evidence that the shaping was wrong, not that the team was slow. So the exception is not a loophole; it is a diagnostic that routes the failure to the right place.

Caveat: these three questions are not Singer's framework. They are assembled from §1.2, §2.2 and §3.5 to serve this mission. Repeating them to someone as "Shape Up's diagnostic" would overstate the source.

The adoption guard

Once you can diagnose a system, the temptation is to import whatever it has that you lack. That is where the cargo cult starts, and the tell is subtle: the mechanism is genuinely good and you genuinely want it. Both can be true while the adoption is still hollow.

The test is one question: does the problem the mechanism solves exist here, independent of the source? If the only evidence that you need it is that Basecamp found it useful, you are copying a shape. If you can point at your own overruns first, and reach for the mechanism second, you are absorbing.

Now you run it

Target: Control-Gated Slices (core ADR-0086) — vertical slices governed by control gates, with Success Criteria expressed as MOE→MOP→TPM, a Brassboard/shadow stage for any control that acts, and RIDM promotions that are data-gated on measured TPMs. (R4)

1. Run Q1 on Control-Gated Slices. What does it hold still?

Scope and quality are fixed; time floats. Success Criteria are measured TPMs, and a slice advances when they clear — however long that takes. Which makes the two methods duals: same unit of work, opposite fixed variable. That matters practically, because importing "appetite" onto a slice while keeping TPM-gated promotion produces work that is time-boxed and quality-gated with no rule for which one yields.

2. Run Q3. A slice has been open far longer than anyone expected. What does the method say happens by default?

Nothing fires — elapsed time isn't a gate condition. Option A is the trap, and it is a real rule: on TPM breach a control stays in Brassboard, gets corrective action, and re-samples. (R4) But that is a stopping rule for failing a measurement, not for running long. Conflating the two is the difference between an imagined gap and a real one — and it is why "the fleet has no circuit breaker" is precise rather than sloppy: it has one for breach, none for time.

3. When is importing the circuit breaker here an absorption rather than a cargo cult?

The local problem has to exist first. B is the cargo-cult answer in its most respectable dress — evidence about their system is not evidence about yours. Here the local evidence happens to be abundant and independent: slices flipped ready that stay open, 28 open hooks, TD-006's closure gap. Note also that this fleet already wrote the rule at a different altitude — "a harness that is never invoked is theatre with a log file, and retiring it is the loop working" (R5) — which turns the question from "should we adopt this?" into "why does it only apply to harnesses?"

Go deeper — one source

Shape Up §3.5, "Decide When to Stop" — basecamp.com/shapeup/3.5-chapter-14, archived at raw/shape-up-ryan-singer.md SECTION 16. It is the chapter where the method stops asserting and starts specifying: compare-down-to-baseline, scope hammering, and the two conditions for extending. If you read one chapter of this book properly, this is the one.

One thing this lesson cannot tell you: whether any of it transfers outside Basecamp. The book is n=1 by its own account, appendix §4.1 is unread, and no field evidence has been gathered. Treat "it works" as unsourced in both directions.
