Telling absorption from cargo cult

Lesson 0001 · pocock-absorb-vs-cargo-cult · for reviewing verdicts you didn't write

You have thirty-six verdicts about Matt Pocock's skills library sitting in your own repository — D1 through D36, across three decisions/adopt-stack/ records. Agents produced them. You ratified the slates. The uncomfortable question is whether you could grade one.

That is not a knowledge gap. You can read them. It is a discrimination gap, and the distance across it is short — because the records already contain the answer, in a form nobody wrote down.

The thing that generates the practices

Start with what Pocock says he is optimizing for, because everything else is downstream of it: predictability — "the agent taking the same process every run, not producing the same output — is the root virtue; every lever below serves it." (D26)

Notice what that objective licenses. If the goal were good output, you would tune prompts. Because the goal is a stable process, you build structure that constrains the process: information hierarchies, completion criteria, leading words, hooks. This is why his work reads as harness design rather than prompt engineering — and it is the same instinct that made your own deposit step a script instead of a paragraph asking nicely.

Cargo cult is what happens when you copy the levers without the objective. The levers still look right. They just aren't attached to anything.

The discriminator

Three questions. A real absorption answers all three; adoption-for-form fails the second.

  1. Landing. Does the verdict name the specific local surface the opinion attaches to — a file, a line, an existing check?
  2. Independent problem. Can you state the local problem it solves without mentioning the source?
  3. Falsifier. Is there a named measurement or condition under which this verdict would be wrong?

Question 2 is the load-bearing one. "It fits our philosophy" and "his framework is coherent" are both answers about the source. Neither survives the rewrite.

Run it on D28. The opinion is Pocock's router skill, for when user-invoked skills multiply. The verdict is REJECT, and the reason is that the fleet already measured it: a hook-forced suggester scored 84–100% against the router's ~80%. (D28, citing D15 and 2026-07-17-skill-loop-sota) Landing: named. Problem: "we need discovery to work and we measured two mechanisms" — no mention of Pocock required. Falsifier: the number. If the router had won, the verdict flips.

That is a reject carrying evidence, not preference. It is also why rejects are worth more than absorbs when you are auditing: a reject has to argue against a coherent source, so it shows its work.

The tell you can scan for

Now look at the shape of the verdicts rather than their content:

ABSORB, eval-gated D29ties it to an instrument that must pass

REJECT as stated; ABSORB the accounting D27splits the opinion from its mechanism

Every qualifier is the local translation happening in the open. So the scan is cheap: a qualified verdict has usually done the work; a bare ABSORB of a whole shape is where adoption-for-form hides. That is a heuristic, not a law — which the next section is about — but it costs you seconds on output you didn't write.

The hard case, which is the actual lesson

D26 — the predictability opinion above — is a bare ABSORB with no measurement attached. By the scan, it is suspect. Is it?

Work the discriminator. Landing: yes, SKILL.md L7 and a GLOSSARY.md entry. Independent problem: yes, and this is the interesting part — the record says it "names what the fleet's audit apparatus already optimizes for but had no word for." The apparatus predates the reading. The problem is a missing name for an existing thing, which is stateable with no reference to Pocock at all. Falsifier: thin, but real — go look at the audit apparatus and check whether it actually did optimize for process stability. If it didn't, the verdict is retroactive storytelling.

So it passes, and it is still the weakest species of absorption: vocabulary. That is the cheapest thing to adopt and the easiest to justify — and it carries a specific delayed risk. Once the word is in your glossary, you start doing things because the word implies them rather than because you measured a need. The cargo cult doesn't arrive with the verdict. It arrives three decisions later, when someone cites the word as authority.

Which sharpens the heuristic into something you can defend out loud: a bare absorb isn't wrong, it's unfinished — it has taken on a debt to name its falsifier later.

Check yourself

1. An agent returns: "ABSORB — this is the standard pattern in the source and it is internally consistent with their other skills." Which discriminator question does it fail?

The independent problem. Strictly it names no landing and no falsifier either — but "consistent with their other skills" is the diagnostic phrase, because it is an argument about the source. Landing and falsifier can be added by an agent on request; a missing local problem usually means there wasn't one. Reviewing tip: reach for question 2 first, since it is the one that can't be patched.

2. Why does the corpus's reject set carry more information per verdict than its absorb set?

It has to argue against something coherent. Agreeing with a well-made source can be done with no local knowledge at all — that is exactly why absorbs can be hollow. Disagreeing requires you to produce the thing you have that is better, which is local evidence by construction. D28 has to show 84–100% vs ~80% precisely because the router is a reasonable idea.

3. You are reviewing an agent's adopt-stack output and have four minutes. What is the highest-yield thing to do?

Scan for bare absorbs. The qualifiers are the agent showing its translation work, so qualified rows are cheap to trust and expensive to re-derive. Reading the source yourself is the option that raises manual load — the thing this whole lesson is built to avoid. The scan is a filter that turns a full audit into three or four rows worth interrogating.

What to say when someone asks how you work

The articulable version is one sentence: an opinion earns a place only when you can name what it lands on, state the problem it solves without naming its author, and say what would prove it wrong. Everything else — the records, the gates, the numbered verdicts — is bookkeeping that makes that sentence checkable by someone who wasn't there.

This is also, notice, the same structure as the deposit ruling: a mechanism that emits evidence beats a convention that asks nicely, because only one of them can be checked by someone who wasn't in the room.

Going deeper
